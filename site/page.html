Magee, Mississippi · Thirty-three acres

The Thurman Place

Thirty-three acres outside Magee, Mississippi. A pond full of catfish, and a house going up one weekend at a time. Eldridge Lee Thurman saw all of it before any of it was here. This is his vision, and the family that’s building it together.

Thirty-three acres, one family, and one man’s picture of what it could be. Eldridge Lee Thurman drew the plan. His family is the one still building it, together.

He passed away recently. The pond he dug is full. The first house he planned has its roof on and drywall going up inside. The fence line for the next homes is in the ground. This page is how we keep the whole picture in one place: the land, the work, the people doing it, and the man who started it.

Chapter one

The Land

Thirty-three acres in Simpson County, just outside Magee. The pond on the west side, the house on the east, open ground between, red clay underneath. Tap a spot on the map to see it.

The Pond

Dug into red clay at the tree line, and the first thing Eldridge finished. It filled with rain, then with catfish, and it’s where the family still gathers first when they come out.

Chapter two

The Pond

He fed the catfish every evening. Tap the water and they’ll come up.

Red clay, rainwater, and more catfish than anybody has counted.

The pond was the first piece of the plan to become real. Eldridge had it dug at the low edge of the field where the pines start, and he waited on the rain to fill it. Then he stocked it, and then he made feeding those fish the closing chore of every day he was out here.

Stand on the bank at dusk and throw a handful in. The water boils. That part is his.

Your turn

Tap the water

Nobody schedules the trips to the bank. Somebody says let’s go check the pond and the whole group ends up standing at the edge of the clay, reflected in the water.

Chapter four

The Hands

No crew was hired for this. The people in these pictures are family, and every wall went up in their hands.

Weekends, holidays, and the days off nobody could really spare.

The slab got poured, the walls got stood, the roof got shingled, and the fence posts got set by fathers, sons, uncles, cousins, and a couple of kids who mostly carried water. That was the point. Eldridge didn’t want to hire this out. He wanted it built by his own people, so that whoever buys a house out here is buying something the family put up with its own hands.

Every picture on this page was taken by somebody who was also holding a tool that day. All of the photos and videos are in the gallery.

Chapter five · Eldridge Lee Thurman

Eldridge

He saw the pond before the pond. He saw the houses before the first nail.

Eldridge Lee Thurman looked at thirty-three acres of grass and pine outside Magee, Mississippi, and told everybody exactly what was going to happen on it. A pond down at the tree line. A house in the middle. Then more houses across the land, built by the family and sold, each one paying for the next. Work the family could do together, on land that would keep giving back.

He didn’t get to see the last of it. He passed away recently, with the first house dried in and the pond full. But he saw enough to know it was working, and he spent every evening out here doing the one chore he never handed off: a bucket of feed, a walk to the bank, and a few minutes watching the catfish come up.

To the people reading this he was a husband, a father, a friend, a neighbor. Everybody has their own version of him. This land is the one thing all of those versions had in common.

Everything on this land is still being built to his drawing, by the people he drew it with. That was always the other half of the plan. Not just what got built, but who built it, side by side, and what that did for the family.
